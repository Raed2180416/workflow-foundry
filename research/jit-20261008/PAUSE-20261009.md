## 3. The analysis: what the evidence says and where the risk sits

### 3.1 The construction question is now sharply posed, and we have a baseline to beat
Anthropic's release (PRIOR-ART-MANAGED-AGENTS-DW.md) is the strongest form of the alternative: a frontier model writes
an open program per task, with a host that runs it well. Our bet is a cheap model composing from a closed vocabulary
and an evidence-gated library. Evidence for us: 30 constructs cover 97.8% of 1,559 curated workflows (in-sample), and
every W-bed necessity control fails (the keyword rule 0.42–0.65 on R2; amount-only pairing 0.40 wrong on R4), so
structure, not head-count, is what the hard cases need. Evidence against us: C0 from scratch ≈ 60% (A1), and the one
reuse experiment we have (skills) was 0/21 correct. The owner's instruction is to keep our ideology; the proof is arm
(c′) against arm (g) on the same tasks (§4 step 5, owner decision §5.1).

### 3.2 The B4 blocker is the first thing to fix, and it is cheap
Slice-1 profiles refuse 30–55% of in-profile children. A paid ladder run on top of that would measure the profile's
false-refusal rate, not Haiku's construction ability. The slice 2 brief (`_local/flow-v3-slice2-brief.md`) has the fix
(profile JSON-lines features, options, `novelMode: warn`) plus the four mechanisms adopted from the prior art
(run-level resume, per-item failure policy, prefix sharing, phase spans). One agent, $0, gated by E2 record.

### 3.3 The coverage claim failed its own audit, and that is the right order of discovery
97.8% was the census's count of items it did not refuse. On 60 stratified hand labels the vocabulary explains 88.4%
(75.0–95.1), and the detectors' rule precision is 0.73 against a bar of 0.85 (AUDIT-L1). Three readings: (i) the closed
vocabulary still explains most of the corpus, and the two readings differ mainly on whether a Node-RED dashboard app is
a refusal (88.4% vs 96.1%), which is the owner's call; (ii) the census tool is a candidate generator and must not be
quoted as a measurement again; (iii) the checklists (L6-a, 6/21 of our own recorded failures named) were mined from
the corpus's concerns, not ours, so the latent-defect claim (D9) gets its lines from DEV-NOTES, FRONTIER-GAPS and the
R8 fault classes first. Order on resume: revise L1 (six detectors out, P37 split, 15 lines in), re-census, re-audit on
a fresh draw with the owner's 10-item blind check, then J1 (`_local/l1b-heldout-coverage-brief.md`) on platforms
outside the corpus. Decision for J1 stays: within 10 points of the (corrected) in-sample figure.

### 3.4 Everything is K = 1, and the power table says what K = 2 can decide
stats.mjs power table: K = 2 lineages decide gaps of 23–52 points on 24-task streams. Smaller gaps need more tasks or
more lineages. Plan every paid comparison at K ≥ 2 and read it with the PREREG rule; a single-lineage result is a
rehearsal, whatever it shows.

### 3.5 Jev's non-independence changes its job, not its presence
Jev agrees with Claude too often to be an independent judge (E1). It remains useful as a cheap *ranker* whose ports
must beat code baselines (JEV-PORTS). Until then, code oracles and guards carry acceptance, which is the design anyway.

### 3.6 Quota and RAM are the real throttle
The owner's 5h window is the brake (rule: no paid run or new agent above ~0.8). Tonight's G6 deviation (3 GB available
with the owner's own GPU jobs running) held at concurrency 2 without faults; the Stage B rate-limit faults were all at
the usage limit. The resume week should budget the window before each paid step (§4 cost column).

### 3.7 The latent-defect heal claim needs real defects
Synthetic planted defects test the sweep's mechanics, not its value. J2 (defect miner over kept solvers) supplies real
in-profile defects; T4b then tests the sweep on them. Both wait on the first accepted solvers from a paid ladder run.
